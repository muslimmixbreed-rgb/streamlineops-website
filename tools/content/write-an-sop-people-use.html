A standard operating procedure (SOP) is simply a written description of the best way to do a task. The problem is that many SOPs are long, out of date and never opened. Here's a format that people actually use.

Start with the right tasks

You don't need an SOP for everything. Write them first for tasks that are:

  • done often, by more than one person;
  • costly or risky if done wrong;
  • done rarely enough that people forget the steps (month-end, for example).

Keep it to one page

If it doesn't fit on one page, it's probably two procedures. A good SOP has six parts:

  1. Title: what the task is, in plain words. "Processing a customer refund", not "Refund Procedure Policy v2".
  2. Purpose: one sentence on why it matters.
  3. Owner: who keeps the SOP up to date.
  4. When to use it: what triggers the task.
  5. Steps: numbered, each one starting with a verb.
  6. Done means: how you know the task is complete and correct.

Write steps people can follow

  • Start each step with an action: "Open", "Check", "Send".
  • One action per step.
  • Say where things are: the folder, the screen, the button.
  • Add a screenshot or photo where words would be slow.
  • Flag the step where mistakes usually happen.

Test it on someone new

Ask someone who hasn't done the task to follow the SOP while you watch, without helping. Every time they hesitate or ask a question, the SOP needs a tweak. This is the single most useful check you can do.

Keep it alive

Put the SOP where the work happens: pinned in the shared drive, printed by the till, or linked from your tracker. Put a "last reviewed" date on it, and when the process changes, change the SOP the same day.

Quick win: pick the task you get asked about most often and write its one-page SOP today. Fifteen minutes now saves those same questions every week.
